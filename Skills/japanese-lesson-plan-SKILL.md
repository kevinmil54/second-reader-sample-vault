# Japanese-Style Lesson Plan (授業研究 Format)

## Overview

This skill converts lesson plans into the format used in Japanese lesson study (jugyō kenkyū / 授業研究). The Japanese research lesson plan is fundamentally different from Western lesson plans in several ways:

1. **It is a research document**, not just an instructional guide. It situates a single lesson within a unit, a developmental trajectory, and a research question about student learning.
2. **It anticipates student thinking** in detail — not as a list of "misconceptions to correct" but as a map of the intellectual territory students are likely to traverse, which the teacher uses to orchestrate discussion.
3. **It plans the teacher's moves in relation to anticipated student responses**, creating a two-column flow that makes the lesson's intellectual structure visible.
4. **It plans board work (板書計画 bansho keikaku)** in advance, treating the board as a cumulative record of the lesson's intellectual journey.
5. **It names key pedagogical moments** using shared professional vocabulary: hatsumon (発問), kikan-shido (机間指導), neriage (練り上げ), matome (まとめ).

## Document Structure

A Japanese-style lesson plan should contain these sections in order. Not all sections will be equally developed for every lesson — adapt to the content. The section names below use both English and Japanese terms. Output in **markdown format** unless the user requests otherwise.

### 1. Title and Context (授業の概要)

- **Lesson title**
- **Course / Grade level**
- **Date and duration**
- **Instructor**
- **Unit**: Where this lesson sits in the sequence (what came before, what comes after)
- **Number of sessions in the unit** and which session this is

### 2. Research Theme (研究テーマ)

The overarching question the instructor/team is investigating about teaching and learning. This is NOT the lesson's learning objective — it is the professional inquiry that motivates the lesson's design choices. For a university seminar, this might be a question about how students build conceptual understanding, change their mental models, or engage with evidence.

Example: *"How can structured comparison of educational systems help students move beyond surface-level cultural comparisons to identify the organizational and cultural conditions that shape teaching expertise?"*

### 3. Goals of the Lesson (本時のねらい)

State what students should understand or be able to do by the end of the lesson. Be specific and observable. Distinguish between:
- **Content goals**: What students should know or understand
- **Process/thinking goals**: What kind of thinking or discussion the lesson aims to develop
- **Disposition goals** (optional): What attitudes or orientations the lesson aims to cultivate

### 4. Background and Rationale (教材研究 Kyōzai Kenkyū)

This is the intellectual heart of the plan — the instructor's study of the "teaching materials" broadly construed. It should include:

- **Why this topic matters** in the context of the course and unit
- **What students already know** (from prior sessions, readings, their own experience)
- **Key concepts and their relationships** — the content territory of the lesson
- **What makes this content difficult or interesting** — where are the conceptual challenges, the surprising ideas, the places where students' prior mental models may be disrupted?
- **How the readings/materials connect** to the lesson's goals
- **What the instructor has learned from teaching this before** (if applicable)

This section should demonstrate that the instructor has done deep homework on the content and on student thinking, not just planned activities.

### 5. Anticipated Student Thinking (予想される生徒の反応)

This is the most distinctive feature of Japanese lesson planning. For each major question or activity, describe:

- **What responses students are likely to give** — including both productive and unproductive ones
- **What prior knowledge or experience** students are likely to draw on
- **What difficulties or confusions** are likely to arise
- **What partial understandings** students may express that can be built upon
- **The range of sophistication** you expect in responses — from surface-level to deep

This section should read like a map of the intellectual landscape students will traverse. It is NOT a list of "wrong answers to correct" — it is a genuine attempt to understand how students are likely to think, so the teacher can plan how to use their thinking productively.

Format anticipated responses with enough specificity that an observer could use them to track what is actually happening in the lesson.

### 6. Flow of the Lesson (本時の展開)

This is the main body of the plan, structured as a **table or parallel-column format** with these columns:

| Time | Phase & Activity | Teacher's Moves (教師の活動) | Anticipated Student Responses (予想される生徒の反応) | Points of Evaluation / Notes (評価・留意点) |
|------|-----------------|---------------------------|----------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|

The phases should use Japanese lesson study terminology where applicable:

- **Introduction / Posing the problem (課題提示)**: Setting up the intellectual problem of the lesson. Includes the **hatsumon (発問)** — the key question that drives student thinking.
- **Individual/group work (自力解決)**: Students working with the material. Teacher engages in **kikan-shido (机間指導)** — purposeful circulation, observing and noting student thinking, selecting and sequencing responses for later discussion.
- **Whole-class discussion — Neriage (練り上げ)**: The "kneading" or "polishing" of ideas through structured discussion. The teacher orchestrates comparison of different student ideas, drawing out connections, contrasts, and deeper principles. This is typically the heart of the lesson.
- **Summary — Matome (まとめ)**: The teacher (or students) consolidate what has been learned. This is NOT simply repeating the learning objective — it should capture the intellectual journey of the lesson and the key insight that emerged.

**Critical formatting principles:**
- The "Teacher's Moves" column should include actual language the teacher might use — key questions, prompts, and transition statements.
- The "Anticipated Student Responses" column should include specific likely responses, not just "students will discuss."
- The "Points of Evaluation" column should note: what to look for as evidence of understanding, potential decision points, and reminders about pedagogical intent.

### 7. Board Plan (板書計画 Bansho Keikaku)

A text-based sketch or description of how the board should look at the end of the lesson (or at key moments). In Japanese practice, the board is a cumulative record — nothing is erased. The board plan should show:

- Where the lesson's key question or problem appears
- Where student ideas will be recorded
- How ideas will be organized to support comparison and synthesis
- Where the summary (matome) will be written

For a text-based representation, use a simple ASCII diagram or describe the spatial layout.

### 8. Evaluation Criteria (評価規準)

How will you know if the lesson achieved its goals? Specify:
- **What to look for during the lesson** (formative indicators)
- **What to look for in student products** (if any)
- **What the closing reflection should reveal** about whether the lesson's intellectual purpose was achieved
- **What would indicate the lesson did NOT achieve its purpose** — this is just as important

### 9. Materials and Preparation (準備物)

List of all materials, technology, handouts, readings, and preparation needed.

### 10. Post-Lesson Reflection Protocol (授業後の検討会)

Include 2-3 observation focus questions that would guide a post-lesson discussion if observers were present. These should connect back to the Research Theme.

## Adaptation Notes

The traditional Japanese lesson study format was developed primarily for K-12 mathematics instruction using a structured problem-solving approach. When adapting it for other contexts (university seminars, non-math subjects, discussion-based courses), keep these principles:

1. **Preserve the anticipation of student thinking.** This is the core value of the format. Even in a discussion-based seminar, you can anticipate what students are likely to say, what connections they're likely to miss, and what will surprise them.

2. **Preserve the parallel structure.** The side-by-side of teacher moves and student responses makes visible the relationship between what the instructor does and what students think. This is valuable regardless of subject.

3. **Adapt the phase names.** "Hatsumon" works for any discipline as the key question that drives the lesson. "Neriage" works whenever students' ideas need to be compared and synthesized. "Matome" works whenever the lesson needs consolidation. "Kikan-shido" applies to any moment when the teacher is observing student work to inform subsequent instruction.

4. **Don't force the format where it doesn't fit.** A lecture segment doesn't need an "anticipated student responses" column — just note what students should be taking away from it. A video viewing doesn't need kikan-shido. Use the format's strengths where they apply.

5. **The research theme matters.** Even if you're not doing formal lesson study with a team, framing the lesson around a genuine inquiry about student learning changes how you plan. It shifts the focus from "what will I do?" to "what will students think, and how will I use their thinking?"

## Output Format

- Default output is **markdown** (.md file)
- Use tables for the Flow of the Lesson section
- Use headers (##, ###) for major sections
- Include Japanese terms in parentheses at first use
- Keep the document readable — the plan should be useful to the instructor, not just formally complete
